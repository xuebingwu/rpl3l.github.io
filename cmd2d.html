Dilated cardiomyopathy, type 2D (CMD2D)

CMD2D (OMIM 619371) is a rare autosomal recessive cardiomyopathy caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous pathogenic variants in RPL3L. It is characterized by neonatal onset of severe dilated cardiomyopathy with rapid progression to cardiac decompensation and death unless the patient undergoes heart transplantation. Heterozygous carriers are unaffected.

Rare — but not hypothetical

Eleven affected families were confirmed as of the 2026 mechanism study (Murphy et al.), with additional case reports since — on the order of fifteen to twenty patients worldwide (Europe, the Americas, the Middle East, and Asia). Because the disease is so new to the literature, the true number of affected children is almost certainly higher: neonatal DCM is common, and RPL3L is not yet routinely tested in every workup.

Clinical presentation

Babies with CMD2D are typically born at term and appear well at birth. Within the first days to weeks of life they present with acute heart failure, often triggered by nothing more than the normal postnatal circulatory transition or a minor illness.

Typical admission findings

  • Respiratory distress, hypotension, poor feeding, irritability
  • Severe metabolic acidosis with elevated lactate
  • Markedly elevated NT-proBNP (values > 35,000 pg/mL reported)
  • Echocardiography: severely dilated left ventricle with severely reduced shortening fraction

Differential diagnosis

Initial presentations are frequently misattributed to sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy or viral myocarditis. Reported cases were confirmed infection- and virology-negative. In any neonate with unexplained severe DCM, biallelic RPL3L variants should be considered, and rapid genetic testing (see below) should be pursued alongside standard workup.

Genetics

CMD2D is caused by biallelic (homozygous or compound heterozygous) pathogenic variants in RPL3L. The reported spectrum is small — 14 alleles across 11 confirmed families, plus additional case reports — and includes:

  • Missense variants in the conserved RPL3 domain, including the two recurrent hotspots c.80G>A; p.Gly27Asp (found on multiple continents) and p.Asp308Asn/p.Asp308Val
  • Frameshift variants (e.g., p.Ala359Glyfs*4, c.1074dupA; p.Ala359fs*6) predicted to trigger nonsense-mediated mRNA decay — true loss-of-function alleles
  • A splice-donor variant (c.1167+1G>A)

Affected individuals typically carry one of two recurrent hotspot missense variants paired with a private (family-specific) allele. Parental carrier testing and genetic counseling are essential; one reported case involved a de novo variant on one allele. Beyond CMD2D, a RPL3L variant (p.Arg242Trp) has been reported in a child with catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, suggesting the gene can contribute to a broader range of cardiac phenotypes.

Mechanism: a dual loss- and gain-of-function disease

The recessive inheritance pattern initially suggested simple loss of function — but the biology is more interesting, and more instructive for therapy:

  • The knockout paradox. Mice lacking Rpl3l show no overt cardiac phenotype or only mild dysfunction, apparently because the ubiquitous paralog RPL3 is upregulated and compensates.
  • Two classes of human variants. Non-hotspot variants phenocopy knockout: RPL3L function is lost, but RPL3 compensation is permitted. Hotspot variants (such as p.Gly27Asp) do something worse:

The hotspot double hit

Hotspot RPL3L variants simultaneously (1) induce nucleolar protein aggregation and disrupt rRNA processing, blocking ribosome biogenesis, and (2) preserve RPL3L's role in repressing RPL3 mRNA via unproductive splicing, preventing the compensatory RPL3 upregulation seen in knockout models. The result is a combined loss-of-function and gain-of-function mechanism that the neonatal heart cannot survive.

This model — built from human genetics, patient tissues, and isogenic cell models — explains why severity depends on the specific allelic combination, and it points directly at a therapeutic hypothesis: restore or mimic the RPL3 compensation pathway.

Diagnosis & management

Diagnosis

  • Clinical recognition: neonatal severe DCM with elevated NT-proBNP, dilated LV, reduced shortening fraction
  • Confirmation by whole-exome sequencing or targeted RPL3L sequencing showing biallelic pathogenic variants; ACMG/AMP criteria have been applied to both missense and frameshift alleles
  • Exclusion of infection and viral myocarditis; parental segregation studies

Management

  • Intensive heart-failure support (e.g., milrinone), transitioning to oral therapy (digoxin, diuretics, ACE inhibition, and others as clinically indicated)
  • Heart transplantation is currently the only definitive life-saving treatment
  • One reported infant managed medically showed improved cardiac function after six months — suggesting outcomes may vary and medical bridge therapy deserves systematic study
  • Genetic counseling for families: recessive inheritance, 25% recurrence risk per pregnancy for carrier parents; prenatal/preimplantation genetic testing is possible once familial variants are known
