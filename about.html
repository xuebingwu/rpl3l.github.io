What is RPL3L?

RPL3L (ribosomal protein L3-like) is a human gene on chromosome 16p13.3 that spans 10 exons (transcript NM_005061) and encodes a 407–amino acid protein of the universally conserved uL3 ribosomal protein family (UniProt Q92901). It is one of the rare "non-canonical" ribosomal protein genes: a paralog of a core ribosomal protein with a highly specialized tissue distribution.

The heart has its own ribosome

Ribosomes are the molecular machines that translate mRNA into protein. The core protein RPL3 is found in the large (60S) ribosomal subunit of essentially every cell in the body. In striated muscle — cardiac muscle and skeletal muscle — RPL3 is replaced by its paralog RPL3L, producing a muscle-specific ribosome.

Why muscle needs its own ribosome is an open question, but the biology is clear: RPL3L-containing ribosomes regulate muscle function, RPL3L expression is dynamically responsive to external stimuli, and the protein plays an essential role in myoblast growth and fusion. UniProt annotates RPL3L as an inhibitor of myotube growth and muscle function, consistent with a regulatory rather than purely structural role.

A useful analogy

Think of every ribosome as the same factory model, but the muscle version ships with a different engine — RPL3L instead of RPL3. When that engine is defective, the factory doesn't just slow down; in some cases it actively blocks the backup engine (RPL3) from compensating. That double failure is what makes CMD2D so severe (see the disease mechanism).

A brief scientific history

Gene & ribosome biology Disease (CMD2D)

  • 1996 — Van Raay et al. clone RPL3L (GenBank U65581) from a human heart cDNA library during mapping of the autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease region at 16p13.3. The 407-aa protein shares 77% identity with RPL3, and Northern blots show transcripts strongly in heart and skeletal muscle (low levels in pancreas) (Genomics 37:172–176).
  • 2016 — RPL3L is shown to be a functional specialist: expression in myoblasts impairs myotube growth (−23% diameter) and fusion, providing the first evidence of ribosome specialization in skeletal muscle.
  • 2018 — Population sequencing associates RPL3L coding variants (p.Ala75Val; c.1167+1G>A) with atrial fibrillation risk (Thorolfsdottir et al., Communications Biology).
  • 2020 — Ganapathi et al. identify biallelic missense RPL3L variants in five children with severe neonatal dilated cardiomyopathy — the first cytoplasmic ribosomal protein implicated in a non-syndromic cardiomyopathy. OMIM assigns CMD2D (619371); Al-Hassnan et al. report a homozygous p.Arg116His case.
  • 2022 — Further case reports confirm autosomal recessive inheritance; an infant with fulminant DCM is successfully bridged to heart transplantation with a Berlin Heart LVAD (Das et al.).
  • 2023 — Ribosome biology comes of age: Rpl3l-knockout mice are viable, with RPL3 upregulation compensating (Milenkovic et al., NAR), and RPL3L-containing ribosomes are shown to determine translation elongation dynamics required for cardiac function (Shiraishi et al., Nature Communications).
  • 2023 — The first Chinese case adds a novel frameshift allele and extends the phenotype to arrhythmias and AV block (Yang et al.).
  • 2026 — Murphy et al. integrate human genetics, patient tissues, and isogenic cell models into a dual loss-of-function / gain-of-function mechanism for hotspot variants; further cases reported (Zhang et al.; Mai et al.).

Open questions

RPL3L biology is young. Key unanswered questions the initiative aims to help answer:

RPL3L biology

  • What are the functional and structural differences between RPL3L-containing and RPL3-containing ribosomes?
  • How is the switch between the two regulated during development and in cardiac hypertrophy?
  • What is the physiological function of this switch?
  • How conserved is the switch across vertebrate evolution?

CMD2D

  • What is the full allelic spectrum of the disease — how many more RPL3L variants remain to be discovered?
  • Are there additional hotspot variants beyond Gly27Asp and Asp308Asn/Val?
  • Do any variants cause disease through mechanisms different from those characterized to date?
  • Why do some variants cause more severe symptoms than others?
  • Do milder RPL3L variants exist in the population, and are they linked to less severe symptoms?
  • What is the connection between CMD2D variants and the variants associated with atrial fibrillation?

Therapeutic development

  • Can the disease be modeled in mice or in patient-derived iPSC-cardiomyocytes?
  • Is RPL3L knockdown a viable therapeutic strategy?
  • How can therapeutics be delivered efficiently to the heart?
  • How early must treatment begin to be effective?
